Eye Strain Complaints Reading Long Text in Dark UI

While dark user interfaces (UI) are often praised for saving battery life and reducing overall brightness in low-light environments, reading long-form text in dark mode frequently causes significant visual discomfort. This article outlines the primary eye-strain complaints users experience when reading sustained text on dark backgrounds, explaining the physiological reasons behind issues such as halation, pupil dilation strain, afterimages, and reduced reading comprehension.

1. Halation and "Ghosting" Effects

The most widespread complaint among dark UI readers is halation, often described as a foggy, blurry, or glowing halo around white letters. When viewing a dark screen, the iris naturally dilates to let in more light. This dilation reduces the eye's depth of field and exaggerates any minor refractive errors in the eye, particularly astigmatism. As a result, the edges of bright text bleed into the dark background, making letters appear fuzzy or doubled.

2. Accelerated Eye Fatigue from Ciliary Muscle Strain

Reading requires the eyes to maintain sharp focus on fine details. Because white text on a black background produces lower visual acuity than black text on a white background, the eye’s ciliary muscles must constantly adjust to keep the blurred text in focus. This continuous micro-focusing leads to rapid muscle fatigue, resulting in aching eyes, tension headaches, and brow ache after relatively short reading sessions.

3. Dryness and Irritation

When visual contrast is difficult to resolve, cognitive load increases. Studies show that higher cognitive load and visual strain cause the blink rate to drop substantially below the normal 15 to 20 blinks per minute. In dark UI, readers struggling with fuzzy text often stare more intently, leading to rapid evaporation of the tear film. This causes classic dry eye symptoms, including burning, grittiness, redness, and a reflex overproduction of watery tears.

4. Lingering Afterimages

Staring at high-contrast white text against a deep black canvas overstimulates localized clusters of photoreceptor cells in the retina. When the user looks away from the screen—or switches to a lighter application—they frequently report seeing "burned-in" horizontal stripes or ghost text floating across their visual field. These negative afterimages can persist for several minutes, causing temporary disorientation and visual distress.

5. Contrast Glare from Pure Black Backgrounds

A frequent design mistake in dark UI is using pure white (#FFFFFF) text on pure black (#000000) surfaces. This extreme 21:1 contrast ratio creates a harsh glare that over-saturates the retina. Readers report that the extreme contrast feels "piercing" to look at, which triggers light sensitivity (photophobia) and forces users to squint to filter out the intensity of the text.

6. Slower Reading Speeds and Loss of Place

Beyond physical discomfort, users often complain that dark UI harms their reading mechanics. Because the visual system processes inverted contrast (light-on-dark) more slowly than positive contrast (dark-on-light), readers frequently report losing their place, skipping lines, or needing to re-read sentences to absorb the material. This repeated scanning compounds the physical strain on the ocular motor muscles.
